Sounds of Polish: The 8 Sounds English Doesn't Have

Master the sibilants, nasal vowels, and consonants that trip up every English speaker — and unlock every Polish word.

Why Polish Looks Terrifying (But Isn't)

Polish looks scary because it uses letters and letter combinations that English doesn't. Words like przepraszam or dźwięk look impossible. But here's the truth: there are only eight sounds in Polish that don't exist in English. Master those eight, and you can pronounce every word in the language. Let's hack them one by one.

The Sibilant Minefield: Hard vs. Soft

Polish has two families of hissing sounds — the hard ones and the soft ones. The hard sounds curl your tongue back (retroflex). The soft sounds push it forward behind your teeth. Practice the pairs: the difference is the tongue position, not the air flow.

SZ
/ʂ/
Hard "sh" — deep, retroflex. Like shushing a cat.
szafa — wardrobe
Ś
/ɕ/
Soft "sh" — light, high. Whisper through your teeth.
śnieg — snow
CZ
/tʂ/
Hard "ch" — like "ch" in "chair" but harder and deeper.
czas — time
Ć
/tɕ/
Soft "ch" — like "cheese" but lighter.
ćma — moth
Ż
/ʐ/
Hard "zh" — like "s" in "measure" but harder.
żaba — frog
Ź
/ʑ/
Soft "zh" — the gentle version of Ż.
źle — badly
DŻ
/dʐ/
Hard "j" — like "j" in "judge" but harder.
dżem — jam
DŹ
/dʑ/
Soft "j" — the lighter version of DŻ.
dźwięk — sound

Practice the minimal pairs: szafa — śnieg. Czas — ćma. Żaba — źle. Hard sounds: tongue back. Soft sounds: tongue forward.

RZ: The Imposter

RZ looks like it should be pronounced "rz" as in the English word "razor." It's not. RZ is pronounced exactly the same as Ż — that deep "zh" sound. Two spellings, one sound. That means less to memorize, not more.

RZ
/ʐ/
Same sound as Ż! Two spellings, one pronunciation.
rzeka — river · morze — sea

The Nasal Vowels: Ą and Ę

Two letters with tails — Ą and Ę. These are nasal vowels, sounds that go through your nose. But here's the secret: in everyday speech, most Poles barely nasalize them. Ą at the end of a word sounds almost like "om." Ę in the middle of a word sounds like "en." Don't obsess over nasality — just approximate and you'll be understood.

Ą
/ɔ̃/
Sounds like "om" in everyday speech. Lightly nasal.
mogą — they can · dziękuję — listen for it
Ę
/ɛ̃/
Sounds like "en" in the middle of words.
ręka — hand

The Dark L: Ł = W

This one surprises every English speaker. The letter Ł — a crossed L — is pronounced like the English "W". Not "L," not "Lu." Just "W." Once you know this, dozens of Polish place names suddenly make sense.

Ł
/w/
Pronounced like English "w". Not "l"!
łapa — paw · Wrocław = "Vro-tsuaf"

The Rolling R

Polish R is rolled — a short trill, like two taps of your tongue against the roof of your mouth. Think of the Spanish R but shorter. Don't stress if you can't roll it perfectly on day one. A single tap R is close enough to be understood.

R
/r/
Rolled, short trill. Like Spanish R but briefer.
rzeka — river · rower — bike

Pro Tip

Don't worry about perfect pronunciation on day one. The goal is being understood, not sounding native. A single-tap R instead of a trill? Fine. A barely-nasal Ą? Fine. Poles will appreciate the effort and help you.

Speaking Drill

Record yourself saying all 20 survival words. Don't read them — say them from memory if you can. Compare your pronunciation to the IPA guide. Focus on the 8 new sounds: sz, ś, cz, ć, ż/rz, ź, ł, ą/ę. Don't worry about perfection — worry about being understood.
